\section{Census and provenance}\label{app:census}

\subsection{The exact minimum census}

The census definition is frozen: nine distinct positive integer entries;
exactly eight positive squares; exactly seven of the eight ordinary lines
with one common sum; quotient by the geometric $D_4$ action; height equal
to the largest positive square root among the eight squares.

\begin{proposition}[Exact minimum]\label{prop:census}
Under this definition there are $0$ classes of height $\le46$ and exactly
$9$ classes of height $\le47$, of which exactly two have a nonsquare
centre.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Certificate argument]
Two independently implemented exact enumerators in the companion repository
agree: \texttt{census\_reference.py} scans integer progression centres and
common differences, while the census family of \texttt{verify.py} builds
full progressions from root triples and partial ones from root pairs. At
height $46$ there are
twelve full three-square progressions, all with distinct common
differences; two full progressions of one difference are necessary, so the
census is empty. At height $47$ the only difference supporting two full
progressions is $840$:
\[
(1,841,1681)=(1^2,29^2,41^2),\qquad(529,1369,2209)=(23^2,37^2,47^2),
\]
There are five raw partial progressions satisfying the square-height bound.
Two repeat entries when combined with the full progressions and therefore
fail nine-way distinctness:
\[
(841,1681,2521),\qquad (1369,2209,3049).
\]
The only compatible partial progressions are
\[
\begin{gathered}
(121,961,1801),\qquad(169,1009,1849),\\
(256,1096,1936).
\end{gathered}
\]
For each partial progression the six
role assignments collapse to three $D_4$ classes, giving $3\times3=9$; the
last two partials carry their nonsquare in the middle, and assigning either
to the centre role gives one centre-nonsquare class each. The census is
replayed from scratch by the companion verifier (\texttt{verify.py},
census family), and all nine canonical representatives are frozen in its
expected certificate.
\end{proof}

The three shifts $t=9,96,144$ of the $n=1$ carrier antidiagonal
$(265,1105,1945)$ produce exactly the three partial components of the
minimum layer --- stated carefully: only three designated orientations are
literal antidiagonal retractions of the fixed source orientation; the other
six classes are role reassignments. \textbf{The public census is frozen at
the height-47 bound above}; no figure at any other bound is claimed here.

\subsection{Provenance of the centre-true eight-square arrays}

A related catalogued family must not be confused with the $(8,7)$ metric:
the \emph{centre-true} arrays with a nonsquare centre $g$, eight distinct
square entries around it, magic sum $3g$, and \textbf{six} magic lines
(three rows, middle column, both diagonals; the outer columns fail). Seven
such arrays with centres
\[
157441,\;411625,\;786361,\;1199809,\;2033641,\;12138289,\;19953649
\]
circulated in an earlier phase of this project as if new. A source audit
corrected the record: Brown's note on automedian triangles and magic
squares~\cite{BRO} already lists, for each of these seven centres, the six
roots of the top and bottom rows. What this project adds is only the square
middle-pair completion and the six-line arrangement; whether that
completion is itself absent from the literature has not been source-locked,
and no novelty is claimed. For example, at $g=157441$:
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
421^2 & 49^2 & 541^2\\
481^2 & g & 289^2\\
149^2 & 559^2 & 371^2
\end{pmatrix},
\]
whose six stated lines each sum to $3g=472323$. Brown also states that a
Parker centre root needs more than two distinct primes $1\bmod4$; that
statement is non-refereed and its proof scope is unaudited here, but it
blocks any ``first centre-prime obstruction'' wording, and none appears in
this paper.
